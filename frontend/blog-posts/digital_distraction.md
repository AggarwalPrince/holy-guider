Most of us spend our days staring into glowing rectangles, absorbing the opinions, tragedies, and advertisements of the entire globe. We scroll to numb ourselves, but it often leaves us feeling more hollow and scattered than before. 

Long before smartphones existed, spiritual traditions recognized the danger of taking in too much noise. They all share a practice that we desperately need today: the art of intentional withdrawal.

## Hinduism: gathering your senses

Yoga philosophy describes a practice called *Pratyahara*, which translates to the withdrawal of the senses. It is the understanding that our minds are constantly feeding on whatever our eyes and ears consume. If you consume chaos, your mind becomes chaotic. To find peace, you have to temporarily close the gates. You don't have to move to a cave, but you do have to occasionally turn off the feed.

This noise often causes a subtle, buzzing anxiety. We explore how to quiet it in [Scripture for Anxiety: Calming Teachings from Hinduism, Christianity, Buddhism and Islam](/blog/scripture-for-anxiety-across-religions/).

**Try today:** Put your phone in a drawer, out of sight, for exactly 30 minutes. Notice how many times your brain instinctively reaches for it. 

## Christianity: the desolate places

Throughout the Gospels, Jesus is constantly surrounded by people demanding his attention. His response is to regularly "withdraw to desolate places to pray." He steps away from the noise to remember the voice of God. The Christian tradition values silence not because it is empty, but because it creates the space necessary to hear what actually matters. 

**Try today:** The next time you are waiting in line or sitting in a parked car, resist the urge to pull out your phone. Just stand there and let the moment be empty.

## Buddhism: fasting from input

Buddhism often speaks of the "monkey mind"—a mind that endlessly swings from one branch of distraction to the next, desperate for stimulation. Scrolling is just the modern version of this craving. Buddhist practice teaches that true contentment is found not by feeding the craving with more information, but by sitting still long enough for the craving to eventually settle on its own.

**Try today:** Eat one meal, or even just drink a cup of coffee today, with absolutely no screens, podcasts, or music playing in the background.

## Islam: the practice of retreat

In Islam, there is a practice called *I'tikaf* (spiritual retreat), traditionally done in the final days of Ramadan, where one unplugs from worldly affairs to focus entirely on the Divine. But the principle can be applied daily. The Quran also emphasizes guarding one's eyes and ears from useless or harmful matters. Silence and limiting what you look at are seen as shields for a fragile heart.

**Try today:** Unfollow or mute three accounts on social media that make you feel inadequate, anxious, or rushed.
