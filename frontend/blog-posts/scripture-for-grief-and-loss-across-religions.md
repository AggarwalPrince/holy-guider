Grief has no schedule. It can arrive in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday, months after you thought you were fine. Every major spiritual tradition has had to answer the same question: what do we do when someone we love is gone?

None of these teachings will make the pain disappear, and they don't claim to. What they offer is company, and a way to carry the weight.

## Hinduism: the self is more than the body

The Bhagavad Gita opens its teaching with a grieving man. Arjuna is overwhelmed at the thought of losing people he loves. Krishna's answer, in chapter 2, is that the deepest part of a person is not born and does not die, and that wise people do not stay stuck mourning what changes.

This is not a call to feel nothing. Many readers find it comforting as a different way of seeing: the relationship was real, and the person's essence is not erased by their absence.

For more on this way of seeing, read our guide: [7 Bhagavad Gita Teachings for Anxiety and Overthinking](/blog/bhagavad-gita-quotes-for-anxiety/).

**Try today:** Sit quietly and recall one quality of the person that you can still carry forward, such as their humor, patience or generosity.

## Christianity: God is near to the brokenhearted

The Bible does not rush grief. In the Gospel of John, Jesus weeps at the death of his friend Lazarus, even though he knows what is about to happen. The Psalms are full of raw lament, and Psalm 34 says God is close to the brokenhearted. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus says those who mourn will be comforted.

The message is that sorrow is not a failure of faith. It is permitted, and you are not alone in it.

**Try today:** Read a psalm of lament slowly, such as Psalm 13 or Psalm 88, and let it say what you cannot.

## Buddhism: loss is part of being alive

A well-known Buddhist story tells of Kisa Gotami, a mother who lost her child and begged the Buddha for medicine to bring him back. He asked her to bring a mustard seed from a house where no one had ever died. She went door to door and found none. Through that search she came to see that loss is shared by everyone.

The teaching is not "get over it." It is that you are part of something very human, and that compassion for yourself and others can grow from that.

**Try today:** Spend a few minutes wishing peace to yourself and to everyone else who is grieving today.

## Islam: we belong to God, and we return to Him

In the Quran, those who face loss are encouraged to say that we belong to God and to Him we return (Quran 2:156). The Quran also promises that with hardship comes ease (94:5-6), and that no soul is given more than it can bear (2:286).

Muslims are encouraged to be patient (*sabr*), which does not mean silent. It means continuing to turn toward God while you hurt.

**Try today:** Make a quiet dua, a prayer in your own words, for the person you lost.
