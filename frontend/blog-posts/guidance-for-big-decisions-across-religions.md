Choosing a career, ending a relationship, moving cities, taking a risk: big decisions are stressful because we can't see how they will turn out. People have always turned to faith for help at moments like these, and the major traditions have surprisingly similar advice. Slow down, ask for wisdom, listen to others, and then act.

Here is how four traditions approach a hard choice, followed by a simple method you can try tonight.

## Hinduism: think it through fully, then choose freely

The Bhagavad Gita is built around a decision. Arjuna stands between two armies, unable to act. Krishna spends the whole conversation helping him see his situation clearly: what his duty is, what he's attached to, and what he's afraid of. At the end, Krishna doesn't command him. He asks him to reflect on everything fully and then choose as he sees fit.

The lesson is that guidance is meant to sharpen your own judgment, not replace it. The Gita also advises acting well while loosening your grip on the result, which we cover in [7 Bhagavad Gita Teachings for Anxiety and Overthinking](/blog/bhagavad-gita-quotes-for-anxiety/).

## Christianity: ask for wisdom and seek counsel

Two themes run through the Bible's advice on decisions. The first is asking for wisdom. The book of James says anyone who lacks it should ask God, who gives generously. Proverbs encourages trusting rather than leaning only on your own understanding. The second is listening to others. Proverbs also notes that plans fail without advice and succeed with many advisers.

So the pattern is prayer plus counsel: bring the question to God, and bring it to wise people who know you.

## Buddhism: test it against what causes harm and wellbeing

In the Kalama Sutta, the Buddha tells a group of villagers not to accept a teaching just because of tradition, authority or reputation. Instead, they should look at what actually happens: does this lead to harm and suffering, or to benefit and wellbeing? Part of the Eightfold Path is also "right intention", checking whether a choice comes from greed, ill will or fear, or from kindness and clarity.

This gives you a practical test: ask what each option would do to you and to the people around you, and check your motive honestly.

## Islam: pray for guidance and consult others

Muslims have a specific practice for decisions called *istikhara*. It is a short voluntary prayer followed by a supplication asking God to guide the person toward what is good and to turn them away from what isn't. It is traditionally paired with *shura*, or consultation. The Quran praises conducting affairs by mutual consultation, and the Prophet is told to consult others in matters.

As with the other traditions, the answer isn't meant to come in isolation. It combines prayer, advice, and your own careful thinking.

## A simple 5-step method that borrows from all four

1. **Name the decision in one sentence.** Vague worries make everything feel bigger. Write down exactly what you're choosing between.
2. **Pause.** Give yourself a fixed window, a few days if you can, and don't decide in the middle of panic or excitement.
3. **Check your motive.** Ask whether you're moving toward something you value or away from something you fear.
4. **Ask two or three wise people.** Pick people who know you and will be honest, not just agreeable.
5. **Choose, then let go of the need to be certain.** Take the best-informed step, and accept that no one gets a guarantee.

If it helps, pair this with a daily quiet moment. We explain how in [How to Start a Daily Spiritual Reflection Practice](/blog/how-to-start-a-daily-spiritual-reflection-practice/). And if worry is what's blocking you, see [Scripture for Anxiety: Calming Teachings from Hinduism, Christianity, Buddhism and Islam](/blog/scripture-for-anxiety-across-religions/).

A note on all of this: these are summaries in our own words, not quotations from a particular translation. If a passage speaks to you, read it in full in a translation you trust, ideally alongside a teacher or community from that tradition. For major medical, legal or financial decisions, spiritual reflection is a complement to professional advice, not a replacement for it.
