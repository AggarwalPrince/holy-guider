# Diwali Across Hindu, Jain and Sikh Traditions: Meaning, History, and Related Observances

Every autumn, around the new-moon night (*Amavasya*) of the lunisolar month of *Kartika*, hundreds of millions of clay lamps (*diyas*) illuminate homes, temples, gurdwaras, and streets across South Asia and the global diaspora. Known widely as Diwali or *Deepavali*—derived from the Sanskrit *Dīpa* (lamp or light) and *Āvali* (a continuous row)—the season is marked by prayer, family gatherings, shared sweets, and acts of generosity[cite: 3].

Yet Diwali does not tell one single, uniform story[cite: 3]. Across South Asia, distinct religious traditions—**Hinduism, Jainism, and Sikhism**—observe major sacred milestones on or around this new moon, while neighbouring Himalayan communities mark related autumn festivals of light with their own unique histories and meanings[cite: 3]:

* **For many Hindus**, Diwali celebrates the triumph of *Dharma* (righteous order), the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya, Krishna’s victory over Narakasura, and the worship of Goddess Lakshmi or Goddess Kali[cite: 3].
* **For Jains**, the night commemorates the ultimate spiritual liberation (*Nirvana* or *Moksha*) of Lord Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara[cite: 3].
* **For Sikhs**, the season is observed as *Bandi Chhor Divas*, honoring Guru Hargobind Sahib’s release from imprisonment at Gwalior Fort and his advocacy that secured the freedom of 52 captive princes[cite: 3].
* **Among Newar and Himalayan communities in Nepal**, the five-day festival of *Tihar* (*Swanti*) takes place during the same seasonal window[cite: 3]. Observed by both Newar Hindu and Newar Buddhist households, it includes *Mha Puja* on the fourth day—centering on honouring oneself and marking the *Nepal Sambat* New Year—before concluding with *Bhai Tika* on the fifth day[cite: 3].

Honoring these traditions means understanding each one on its own theological and historical terms, separating community traditions from modern contemplative reflections so we can approach the season with both accuracy and depth[cite: 3].

## 1. Hindu Traditions: Sacred Narratives, Regional Diversity, and Inner Reflection

There is no single story that explains every Hindu celebration of Diwali[cite: 3]. Instead, the festival is observed in diverse ways across India and the diaspora, with emphasis varying by region, family custom, and religious lineage[cite: 3].

### The Epic and Regional Anchors
* **The Return to Ayodhya (North & Central India):** In many North Indian traditions, Diwali is associated with Lord Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana returning to Ayodhya after 14 years of exile and the defeat of Ravana[cite: 3]. While Rama’s return is recorded in the *Valmiki Ramayana*, the familiar custom of illuminating homes and streets with rows of earthen lamps to celebrate this homecoming belongs to later devotional and Puranic traditions (and vernacular retellings such as Tulsidas’s *Ramcharitmanas*). Similarly, in some regions, devotees also remember the return of the Pandavas in the *Mahabharata* after their exile.
* **Victory Over Narakasura (South & Western India):** On the day preceding the main new-moon night (*Naraka Chaturdashi*), traditions across Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Goa, and Maharashtra commemorate Lord Krishna and Satyabhama defeating the tyrannical demon king Narakasura[cite: 3].
* **Lakshmi Puja and Kali Puja:** For many Hindu households, *Lakshmi Puja* is the central Diwali observance, associated with prosperity, well-being, and auspiciousness (*Shri*), alongside the worship of Lord Ganesha for wisdom and auspicious beginnings[cite: 3]. Around the same new-moon period in West Bengal, Odisha, Assam, and Mithila, *Kali Puja* (*Shyama Puja*) is observed, honoring the fierce Mother Goddess through distinct liturgical and regional traditions[cite: 3].

### What Does the Lamp Symbolize? A Contemporary Contemplative View
A *diya* is a small lamp, traditionally made from clay and filled with oil or ghee with a cotton wick[cite: 3]. While not a single canonical explanation shared by every Hindu school, many contemporary spiritual teachers and seekers use the physical components of the lamp as a prompt for personal reflection[cite: 3]:

* **The Clay Vessel:** Molded from the earth, the bowl can remind us of the limits, fragility, and responsibilities of embodied human life[cite: 3].
* **The Oil or Ghee:** In Sanskrit, the word *Sneha* means both "oil" and "affection/tenderness." It can represent the habits, intentions, and care that sustain our daily actions[cite: 3].
* **The Cotton Wick:** Suggests the mental discipline required to focus our scattered energy in one direction[cite: 3].
* **The Flame:** Symbolizes understanding, hope, and spiritual discernment[cite: 3].

### “From Darkness to Light”
A well-known prayer from the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* (1.3.28) is frequently recalled during Diwali reflections[cite: 3]:

> *"Asato mā sad gamaya, tamaso mā jyotir gamaya, mṛtyor mā amṛtaṃ gamaya."*  
> **("Lead me from the unreal to the Real; lead me from darkness to Light; lead me from mortality to the Deathless.")**[cite: 3]

A related image appears in the *Bhagavad Gita* (10.11), where Krishna describes dispelling the darkness born of ignorance with the luminous lamp of knowledge (*Jñāna-dīpena bhāsvatā*). In a personal Diwali reflection, "darkness" can serve as a metaphor for confusion, harmful habits, or egoic reactivity—inviting us to consider both the kind of outer prosperity we seek and the inner clarity we need to cultivate[cite: 3].

## 2. Jain Diwali: Mahavira’s *Nirvana* and the Path to Liberation

For the global Jain community, Diwali commemorates the final liberation (*Nirvana* or *Moksha*) of **Lord Mahavira**, the 24th Tirthankara[cite: 3]. Jain tradition associates this event with **Pawapuri**, in present-day Bihar, on the new-moon night of *Kartika*[cite: 3].

The exact historical year is a matter of traditional chronology rather than a single universally settled modern date[cite: 3]. The **Śvetāmbara** Jain tradition commonly places Mahavira’s *Nirvana* in **527 BCE** (forming the basis of the *Vira Nirvana Samvat* calendar), whereas **Digambara** chronology gives a different traditional date, and modern academic historians propose alternative timelines[cite: 3]. You can explore these calendar traditions in detail at [Jainpedia: Diwali](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/practices/festivals/divali/) and [Jainpedia: Chronology](https://jainpedia.org/resources/chronology/)[cite: 3].

### Why Are Lamps Lit in Jain Tradition?
According to traditional Jain accounts preserved in the *Kalpa Sutra*, eighteen federated kings of the Kashi and Kosala republics—along with the Mallakis and Lecchakis—were present at Pawapuri when Mahavira attained liberation, and instituted an illumination of lamps, declaring[cite: 3]:

> *"Since the Light of Intelligence is gone, let us make an illumination of material matter!"*[cite: 3]

In a separate but closely linked Jain canonical tradition, the *Uttaradhyayana Sutra* is revered as the text preserving Mahavira’s final teachings delivered before his passing, and is frequently recited by monastics and laypeople during the Diwali period.

Together, these traditions connect external illumination with the memory of a teacher’s path and the responsibility of the community to carry spiritual practice forward[cite: 3]. Rather than purely festive celebration, Jain teachings place deep emphasis on liberation from karmic bondage and ethical disciplines such as[cite: 3]:

1. **Ahimsa:** Non-violence and the minimization of harm to living beings—a foundational vow applied with great rigor by monastics and practiced across daily life by laypeople according to their capacity[cite: 3].
2. **Aparigraha:** Limiting attachment, greed, and possessiveness[cite: 3].
3. **Anekantavada:** Recognizing that reality is multifaceted and that no single partial human viewpoint captures the entirety of truth[cite: 3].

Jain Diwali practices vary across communities and families[cite: 3]. Many Jains fast, visit temples, listen to scriptural recitations, meditate, or reflect on Mahavira’s life, while other families also hold social gatherings and festive meals[cite: 3]. In addition, some Jains avoid or limit fireworks during Diwali because of concerns about harming living organisms and the principle of *Ahimsa*[cite: 3].

### Gautama Swami and Non-Attachment
Jain tradition also associates the morning immediately following Diwali—the beginning of the Jain New Year (*Pratipada*)—with **Indrabhuti Gautama (Gautama Swami)**, Mahavira’s chief disciple, attaining *Kevala Jnana* (perfect, omniscient knowledge)[cite: 3]. 

According to the traditional account, despite his immense discipline, Gautama Swami’s deep emotional attachment to his teacher’s physical presence had been the final subtle barrier holding him back[cite: 3]. After working through his grief over Mahavira’s passing and overcoming that final possessive attachment, he attained *Kevala Jnana* at dawn[cite: 3]. As a contemporary reflection, Gautama’s story invites us to ask: *Can I care deeply about people and responsibilities in my life while accepting that I cannot control every outcome or prevent change?*[cite: 3]

## 3. Sikh Tradition: *Bandi Chhor Divas* and Freedom for Others

Sikhs celebrate **Bandi Chhor Divas** around the same time as Diwali[cite: 3]. The name translates as **"the day of liberation of prisoners,"** remembering the sixth Sikh Guru, **Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji**, and his release from imprisonment at Gwalior Fort alongside 52 detained hill princes (*Rajas*)[cite: 3].

Historical accounts differ on chronology[cite: 3]. The **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC)**, a premier Sikh religious institution, describes the release of Guru Hargobind and the 52 princes and records his arrival in Amritsar around Diwali, giving a release date of **1612 CE**, whereas other popular historical accounts often cite **1619 CE**[cite: 3]. Rather than presenting one year as universally agreed, readers can consult the institutional biography directly at the [SGPC Biography of Sri Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji](https://new.sgpc.net/ten-guru-sahibs/guru-hargobind-sahib/)[cite: 3].

### The Meaning of Liberation and the 52 Princes
According to Sikh tradition, when the Mughal Emperor Jahangir ordered Guru Hargobind’s release from Gwalior Fort, the Guru refused to leave alone and advocated for the release of the 52 captive princes held in the fort[cite: 3]. When the Emperor stipulated that only those who could hold onto the Guru’s garment could go free, a special cloak (*Chola*) was prepared with 52 tassels (*kaliyan*), allowing all 52 rulers to walk out of captivity alongside the Guru—earning him the title *Bandi Chhor* (The Liberator)[cite: 3].

While readers can distinguish between religiously meaningful tradition and what can be independently verified about every historical detail, the ethical lesson at the heart of *Bandi Chhor Divas* is unmistakable: **freedom should never be understood purely as a selfish, individual benefit**[cite: 3].

Sikhs mark the occasion through prayer, *Kirtan* (sacred music), visits to gurdwaras, community service (*Langar*), and the illumination of the *Harmandir Sahib* (Golden Temple) in Amritsar[cite: 3]. This synthesis of inward spiritual devotion and outward social responsibility builds directly upon the foundational teachings of Guru Nanak—which you can explore in depth in our article on **[Guru Nanak’s Three Pillars of an Honest Life: Naam Japna, Kirat Karni & Vand Chhako](/blog/guru-nanak-three-pillars-honest-life)**.

### A Reflection on Freedom and Service
A personal reflection inspired by *Bandi Chhor Divas* might ask[cite: 3]:
* *Am I using my freedom, skills, and opportunities to help others who are struggling?*[cite: 3]
* *Can I stand up for someone being treated unfairly?*[cite: 3]
* *Does my personal spiritual practice make me more compassionate and useful to the world around me?*[cite: 3]

## 4. Buddhist and Himalayan Communities: Related Seasonal Festivals, Not One Universal Diwali

Buddhism encompasses diverse traditions across Asia, and **Diwali is not a universal Buddhist festival**[cite: 3]. However, specific communities in the Himalayan region celebrate major seasonal festivals of light during the same autumn period, and these observances deserve to be understood in their own cultural context[cite: 3].

### *Tihar* (*Swanti*), *Mha Puja*, and *Bhai Tika* in Nepal
Nepal’s **Tihar** (known as **Swanti** among the Newar community of the Kathmandu Valley) is a five-day festival of lights with distinct daily observances honoring animals, auspiciousness, the inner self, and family bonds[cite: 3]:
* **Days 1–3:** Include honoring crows (*Kag Tihar*), dogs (*Kukur Tihar*), cows (*Gai Tihar*), and observing *Lakshmi Puja*.
* **Day 4 — *Mha Puja*:** Among Newar communities in Nepal (practiced across both Newar Buddhist and Newar Hindu households), **Mha Puja** is observed on the fourth day of Tihar[cite: 3]. This distinctive Newar ritual centres on honouring and purifying oneself—recognizing the dignity of one's own body and inner consciousness before intricate geometric mandalas—and marks the beginning of the **Nepal Sambat New Year**[cite: 3].
* **Day 5 — *Bhai Tika* (*Kija Puja*):** The fifth and final day of Tihar culminates in *Bhai Tika*, where sisters and brothers honour and bless one another for longevity and protection.

The Nepal Tourism Board outlines the full five-day structure and the Newar observance of *Mha Puja* in its [Official Tihar Festival Guide](https://ntb.gov.np/en/tihar)[cite: 3]. Describing these as Nepalese and Newar traditions respects their unique heritage rather than conflating them with a universal Buddhist Diwali ritual[cite: 3].

### Emperor Ashoka and Buddhist Ethics
In parts of India, another observance—*Ashoka Vijayadashami*—commemorates the Mauryan Emperor Ashoka turning toward moral reflection and the Buddha’s *Dhamma* after the violence of the Kalinga War[cite: 3]. However, Ashoka’s transformation is a separate historical observance associated with particular Buddhist communities (falling twenty days prior to Diwali, on Dussehra) and is not the same event as Diwali[cite: 3]. Even so, Buddhist ethical reflections—reducing greed, hatred, and delusion while cultivating mindfulness and compassion—offer a resonant voice in any broader contemplation on inner light[cite: 3].

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Do Hinduism, Jainism, Sikhism, and Buddhism all celebrate Diwali for the same reason?
No[cite: 3]. Diwali is important across many Hindu, Jain, and Sikh communities, but its historical and theological meaning differs[cite: 3]. Jains commemorate Lord Mahavira’s *Nirvana*; Sikhs observe *Bandi Chhor Divas*; and Hindu traditions encompass multiple regional narratives and rituals[cite: 3]. In the Himalayan region, some Buddhist and Hindu communities celebrate related seasonal festivals such as Nepal’s *Tihar*, though there is no single universal Buddhist Diwali observance[cite: 3].

### Why is Diwali associated with a new-moon night (*Amavasya*)?
In traditional Hindu and Jain lunisolar calendars, the central night of Diwali falls on *Kartika Amavasya*, the new-moon phase when the moon is not visible in the night sky[cite: 3]. Calling it a new-moon night is more astronomically accurate than calling it "the darkest night of the year"[cite: 3].

### Is Mahavira’s Nirvana date definitely 527 BCE?
No single historical date is universally accepted across all traditions and scholars[cite: 3]. The Śvetāmbara Jain tradition commonly uses 527 BCE, whereas Digambara chronology uses a different traditional date, and modern academic chronologies also vary[cite: 3].

### What is the connection between Diwali and *Bandi Chhor Divas*?
*Bandi Chhor Divas* is a Sikh observance coinciding with the Diwali season that remembers Guru Hargobind Sahib’s release from Gwalior Fort and the tradition that he secured the release of 52 other captive princes[cite: 3]. While celebrated around the same calendar window with lamps and *Kirtan*, its religious and historical meaning is distinct from Hindu Diwali narratives[cite: 3].

### Is *Mha Puja* a universal Buddhist Diwali ritual?
No[cite: 3]. *Mha Puja* is a Newar observance in Nepal that takes place on the fourth day of the five-day *Tihar* (*Swanti*) festival, marking the *Nepal Sambat* New Year and focusing on honouring oneself[cite: 3]. Because Newar religious life encompasses both Hindu and Buddhist traditions, *Mha Puja* is best understood as a specific Newar cultural and spiritual ritual rather than a universal Buddhist observance[cite: 3].
