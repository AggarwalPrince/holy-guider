Anger is not a sin or a flaw. It is a signal that something matters to you. Every tradition, though, warns about what happens when we act inside the heat of it. The advice is surprisingly consistent: pause first.

## Hinduism: anger clouds the mind

In chapter 2 of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna traces a chain: dwelling on desires leads to attachment, attachment to anger, anger to confusion, and confusion to loss of clear thinking. The practical lesson is that the time to intervene is early, before the chain gets going.

Related: [7 Bhagavad Gita Teachings for Anxiety and Overthinking](/blog/bhagavad-gita-quotes-for-anxiety/).

**Try today:** Notice where anger starts in your body, such as your jaw, chest or hands, and take three slow breaths there.

## Christianity: a gentle answer

The Book of Proverbs says a gentle answer turns away wrath, while a harsh word stirs it up. The Epistle of James advises being quick to listen and slow to speak and slow to anger. The Bible does not forbid anger, but it asks that it not lead you into harm.

**Try today:** Before replying to something that stung, wait ten minutes and then write the reply you'd be proud of.

## Buddhism: tame the chariot

The Dhammapada compares anger to a speeding chariot. A wise person is the one who can bring it under control, like a skilled charioteer. Buddhist practice treats anger as something to observe with curiosity rather than suppress or obey.

**Try today:** When anger rises, silently label it, "this is anger," and watch it for one minute without acting.

## Islam: restrain your anger

The Quran praises those who restrain their anger and pardon others (3:134). Muslims are also taught to change posture when angry, to seek refuge in God, and to perform wudu (ablution) with cool water, because cooling the body helps cool the temper.

**Try today:** If you are standing, sit down. If you are sitting, step away and wash your face with cool water.
