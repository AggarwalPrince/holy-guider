"Forgive and forget." People say it lightly. If you've been truly betrayed by a partner, a friend, a family member or a business partner, those words can feel almost insulting.

Here is something that tends to help: forgiving someone and trusting them again are two separate things. You can let go of the resentment without handing the person another chance to hurt you. The traditions below are fairly clear about this, even if the language is different. (For a wider look at the theme, see our overview, [How to Forgive](/blog/scripture-on-forgiveness-across-religions/). This post is about the harder case.)

## Christianity: forgiveness is required, trust is rebuilt

Christian teaching is demanding about forgiveness. In the Gospel of Matthew, Peter asks how many times he should forgive, and the answer is far more than he expected. The Lord's Prayer ties our forgiveness to how we forgive others.

The same Gospels, though, describe what to do when someone wrongs you: go to them, name it, and if they will not listen, create distance. Many Christian teachers draw the line like this: forgiveness is a gift you give, trust is something that has to be earned back through changed behavior. Forgiving frees your heart, and it does not oblige you to stay in a harmful situation.

**Try today:** On paper, answer three questions separately. *Am I willing to release this? Do I trust them right now? Do I want contact?* They can have three different answers.

## Buddhism: they were suffering too

Buddhism does not excuse harm, but it asks you to look closely at why people cause it. Someone who betrays is usually acting out of fear, craving or ignorance, which are the very things Buddhism says cause suffering for everyone. Loving-kindness practice (*metta*) starts with wishing yourself well, then slowly extends to people who are difficult, with no requirement to see them or like them.

The Dhammapada says hatred is never ended by hatred. Holding resentment, in this view, is more like holding a hot coal than punishing the other person.

**Try today:** Sit for three minutes and silently say, "May I be free from this pain." Only when that feels true, add "may they also be free from suffering." Skip the second part if you're not there yet.

## Hinduism: forgiveness and karma

The Gita lists forgiveness (*kshama*) among the qualities of a settled, divine nature in chapter 16, and in chapter 12 it praises the person who bears hatred toward no being and is friendly and compassionate. Alongside this sits the idea of karma: what someone does comes back to them in time, and that weight is theirs to carry, not yours to enforce.

For many people this is a relief. You don't need to become the instrument of justice or revenge. You can set the matter down and trust that it is not riding on you.

**Try today:** Write the name of the person and "this is not mine to carry" on a scrap of paper. Then throw it away.

## Islam: pardon is rewarded, justice is still allowed

The Quran says that the repayment for a wrong is a similar wrong, but whoever pardons and makes things right, their reward is with God (42:40). It also asks believers whether they don't want God to forgive them, so they should pardon and overlook (24:22). Islam is careful, though: seeking fairness and protecting yourself are legitimate. Pardon is praised as a high virtue, not demanded as the only response.

**Try today:** If you pray, ask for a heart that can let go, and for clarity about what boundary you need to keep.

## What you're allowed to keep

Across all four traditions, you can forgive and still:

- Keep your distance, or end the relationship.
- Ask for accountability or an honest apology.
- Take practical steps to protect yourself, including legal ones.
- Take as long as you need. Forgiveness is usually a slow process, not a single decision.
