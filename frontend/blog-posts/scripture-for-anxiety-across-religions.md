Worry is one of the oldest human experiences, and almost every spiritual tradition has something to say about it. They use different language, but the same few ideas keep appearing: you can't control everything, the mind can be trained, and peace is something you return to rather than something you achieve once.

Here is how four major traditions speak to an anxious mind, in plain language. Each section ends with a small thing you can try today.

## Hinduism: act fully, loosen your grip on the result

The Bhagavad Gita is set on the eve of a battle, with a warrior frozen by doubt. Its central advice is to do what is yours to do, and to stop clinging to how it turns out. It also treats the mind as something you can train, calling it a friend when disciplined and an enemy when not.

We go deeper on this in [7 Bhagavad Gita Teachings for Anxiety and Overthinking](/blog/bhagavad-gita-quotes-for-anxiety/).

**Try this:** write down the thing you're worried about, then split it into two lists: "what I can do" and "what I can't control." Take one small action from the first list and leave the second alone for today.

## Christianity: bring the worry out loud

The Bible has many passages about worry. In the New Testament, Philippians encourages bringing every concern to God in prayer with gratitude, and describes a peace that guards the heart and mind. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus tells listeners not to be consumed by tomorrow, since each day has enough trouble of its own. The Psalms go further and give worry a voice, with honest lament that doesn't pretend to be fine.

**Try this:** say your worry out loud, or write it as a prayer or a letter, without trying to sound calm. Naming it plainly often loosens it.

## Buddhism: watch the mind, don't obey it

The Dhammapada, a collection of early Buddhist verses, opens with the idea that experience is shaped by the mind. It describes the mind as restless and hard to guard, then compares the wise person to a craftsman who patiently straightens an arrow. The message is that a wandering mind is normal, and that steadiness comes from practice, not from forcing it.

**Try this:** sit for two minutes and follow your breath. When your thoughts pull you away, which they will, note "thinking" and come back. Each return counts as the practice.

## Islam: find rest in remembrance and trust your limits

The Quran speaks of hearts finding rest through the remembrance of God, which is why daily prayer and dhikr (remembrance) are central practices for many Muslims. It also says that no soul is asked to carry more than it can bear, a reassuring line when everything feels like too much.

**Try this:** pause for a few minutes of quiet remembrance, or repeat a short phrase that grounds you, and remind yourself that you are being asked to carry only today's share.

## What the four have in common

- **Name it.** Each tradition invites you to face the worry honestly instead of avoiding it.
- **Narrow your focus to today.** The task is the next right action, not solving the whole future.
- **Practice beats insight.** Peace comes from returning again and again, which is why a short daily habit works. We explain how to start one in [How to Start a Daily Spiritual Reflection Practice](/blog/how-to-start-a-daily-spiritual-reflection-practice/).

A note on reading: these are summaries in our own words, not quotations from any one translation. If a passage speaks to you, read it in full in a translation you trust, ideally with a teacher or community from that tradition. Spiritual reflection can be a comfort, but it is not a replacement for professional help. If anxiety is affecting your daily life, please reach out to a doctor or mental health professional.
