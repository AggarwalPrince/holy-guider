You tell a friend you're having a rough month, and they answer with "everything happens for a reason" or "just focus on the good stuff." You nod, say thanks, and feel a little more alone than before.

We live in a culture that treats sadness as a problem to be solved quickly. The old spiritual traditions didn't. None of them asked people to hide grief, and several of them built whole practices around making room for it. Here is what four of them say.

## Hinduism: pleasure and pain both pass

In chapter 2 of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna describes how contact with the world brings heat and cold, pleasure and pain, and how all of it comes and goes. His advice is to endure it, not to deny it. The person he praises isn't someone who never hurts. It's someone who stays steady while hurting, because they know the feeling won't last forever.

That is a very different instruction from "think positive." It says the pain is real, and so is the fact that it moves. If anxiety is part of what you're carrying, our guide to the [Gita's teachings on anxiety and overthinking](/blog/bhagavad-gita-quotes-for-anxiety/) goes further into this.

**Try today:** When a heavy feeling shows up, say to yourself, "This is sadness, and it is here right now." You don't need to fix it. Just name it.

## Christianity: the long tradition of lament

The Psalms are full of people who are not okay. Psalm 13 asks God how long He will be silent. Psalm 22 begins with a cry of abandonment. Psalm 88 is famous for ending in darkness, without a tidy resolution. These prayers were kept, collected and sung for centuries, which tells you something: honest sorrow was considered a legitimate way to talk to God.

Ecclesiastes says there is a time to weep and a time to laugh, and the New Testament encourages believers to weep with those who weep. It doesn't tell them to cheer each other up.

**Try today:** Read one lament psalm slowly. Then write your own, three sentences of pure honesty, no ending required.

## Buddhism: the second arrow

Buddhism begins with an unflinching observation: life includes suffering. The Buddha used an image that is worth remembering. When you are struck by an arrow, that's the first arrow, the loss or pain itself. Most of us then fire a second one at ourselves: *I shouldn't feel this, something is wrong with me, I should be over it by now.*

The first arrow can't always be avoided. The second one is optional. Letting sadness be sadness, without piling shame on top, is a large part of what the practice is about.

**Try today:** Notice the second arrow. What are you telling yourself about being sad? Try dropping that sentence, and keep only the feeling.

## Islam: a Prophet who grieved

Islamic tradition remembers a year in the Prophet Muhammad's life known as the Year of Sorrow, when he lost both his beloved wife Khadijah and his uncle and protector Abu Talib within a short time. It is remembered as a period of real grief. A well-known account also describes him weeping at the death of his young son Ibrahim, saying that the eyes shed tears and the heart grieves, and that he would say only what pleases God.

For many Muslims this is permission in itself. Tears are not a lack of faith, and a person can be sad and still be devout.

**Try today:** If you pray, tell God exactly how you feel, without trying to sound strong.

## So is positivity the enemy?

No. Hope is a good thing, and so is gratitude. The trouble starts when positivity is used as a way to skip the feeling. All four traditions seem to agree on the order: let the sadness be real first, and let comfort come second.
