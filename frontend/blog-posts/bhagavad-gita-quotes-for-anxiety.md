Anxiety often comes from the mind replaying the past or rehearsing the future — anywhere but the present moment. The Bhagavad Gita, a 700-verse Hindu scripture set on the eve of a battle, spends much of its length on exactly this problem: how to act clearly when the mind is loud.

Here are seven teachings from the Gita that speak directly to an anxious, overthinking mind.

## 1. You are not in control of outcomes — only actions

In chapter 2, Krishna tells Arjuna that he has a right to act, but no entitlement to control how things turn out. Most anxiety is about outcomes we can't control. The counsel isn't to stop caring — it's to stop gripping the result so tightly that it blocks the action itself.

## 2. The mind is both friend and enemy

Chapter 6 describes the trained mind as your greatest ally and the untrained mind as your greatest obstacle. Anxiety isn't a character flaw — it's an untrained mind. The Gita treats steadiness as a skill to build, not a fixed trait you either have or don't.

## 3. An unsteady mind chases every sensation

The Gita compares a mind ruled by the senses to a boat tossed by shifting wind — impossible to navigate with any clarity. When you're anxious, small stimuli feel enormous. The Gita's answer isn't to suppress feeling, but to build steadiness through repeated practice: breath, meditation, and a little distance from the urge to react immediately.

## 4. Grief comes from attachment, not from the thing itself

Krishna tells Arjuna that the wise do not grieve for what is temporary — because everything that arises eventually changes form. Out of context this can sound cold, but the real point is about attachment to permanence: expecting things (health, relationships, plans) to stay fixed. Anxiety often spikes exactly when reality doesn't match an expectation we didn't realize we were holding.

## 5. Your deepest self was never actually at risk

The Gita describes the true self as untouched by birth or death, unburned by fire, unwet by water. Whatever your personal beliefs about the soul, the psychological reframe is useful on its own: your core worth and identity are not as fragile as an anxious moment makes them feel.

## 6. Equanimity is the goal, not constant happiness

Chapter 2 praises the person who meets both pleasure and pain with the same steadiness, calling them ready for real freedom. The Gita never promises a life free of hard days — it promises that a steady mind can meet both good and bad days without being thrown by either.

## 7. Come back to the present, again and again

The disciplined mind, Krishna says, stays even through heat and cold, joy and distress, praise and blame. This isn't achieved once — it's a practice of "coming back," repeated daily, the same way anxiety itself is a habit of leaving the present moment.
