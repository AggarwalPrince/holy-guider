There is a common misconception that being a spiritual or good person means you have to be endlessly accommodating. We often confuse compassion with being a doormat. But when you look closely at the texts of the major religions, you don't find a demand to tolerate abuse, manipulation, or constant disrespect. 

Here is how ancient traditions teach us to love others without losing our own peace.

## Hinduism: compassion and duty

The Bhagavad Gita is literally set on a battlefield where Arjuna has to stand up against relatives who have acted unjustly. Hinduism teaches us to see the divine spark in everyone, but it also heavily emphasizes *dharma* (right action and duty). You can acknowledge the humanity of a difficult person while simultaneously recognizing that your duty is to protect your peace, your family, and your integrity from their chaos. 

If dealing with this person fills you with frustration, we recommend [Scripture for Anger: Calming Teachings from Four Religions](/blog/scripture-for-anger-across-religions/).

**Try today:** Think of one boundary you need to set with a difficult person. Practice saying it out loud in the mirror using a neutral, calm voice. 

## Christianity: love doesn't mean enabling

Jesus famously taught to "turn the other cheek" to an insult, which was a radical call to non-retaliation. But he also told his disciples that if a town did not welcome them or listen to their words, they should "shake the dust off your feet" and leave. He regularly walked away from people who were trying to trap or manipulate him. Loving someone sometimes means refusing to participate in their toxicity.

**Try today:** If someone sends you a text today that feels manipulative or aggressive, give yourself permission to wait a full 24 hours before replying.

## Buddhism: wise compassion vs. idiot compassion

In Buddhist psychology, there is a brilliant concept called "idiot compassion." This is when we let people cross our boundaries just to avoid conflict, secretly hoping we look like the "nice guy." True, wise compassion is sometimes fierce. It is the understanding that letting someone continue to act terribly around you isn't actually good for them, and it certainly isn't good for you. 

**Try today:** Notice if you are saying "yes" to a request today purely out of guilt. If you are, politely change it to a "no."

## Islam: no harm, no reciprocating harm

A foundational principle in Islamic ethics, based on a saying of the Prophet Muhammad, is: "There should be neither harming nor reciprocating harm." You are instructed to repel evil with good, but you are not required to throw yourself into harm's way. If a relationship is deeply toxic, Islamic tradition permits creating physical or emotional distance (*hijrah*) to preserve your faith, sanity, and well-being.

If you are carrying lingering resentment from a toxic dynamic, read [How to Forgive: Wisdom from Four Religions](/blog/scripture-on-forgiveness-across-religions/).

**Try today:** Remind yourself that "No, thank you" is a complete sentence. You do not always owe people a long, agonizing explanation for your boundaries.

## Protecting your peace

Protecting your inner peace is a daily practice. Establishing a [Daily Spiritual Reflection Practice](/blog/how-to-start-a-daily-spiritual-reflection-practice/) can help you stay grounded so that other people's storms don't become your emergencies. 

You can wish people well, pray for their healing, and still politely close the door to them.
